Introduction

What “Adoption” Actually Means

A common misconception is that simply aquiring a pet counts as adoption, including buying from a commercial breeder or pet shop. But actually, adoption has a specific meaning: giving a surrendered or rescued animal a new home.

In fact, formal adoptions account for only a minority of pet acquisitions. Survey results from 2024 show that just 36% of pets were acquired through a shelter, rescue or adoption agency.

Where is adoption happening in Australia?

Victoria has the highest adoption rate per 100,000 people.

That lead makes access the next part of the story: are the places with more adoptions also the places with more rescue organisations?

NSW, Victoria and Queensland have the deepest rescue networks.

The concentration of organisations overlaps with the states recording the most adoption activity, suggesting that local rescue capacity helps shape where adoption can happen.

Who is being adopted?

Before looking at why some pets wait longer, it helps to see who makes up the adoption picture. Dogs and cats account for most recorded PetRescue adoptions, but their paths through the system are not identical.

Cats account for the largest share of recorded adoptions.

The species mix gives us the scale of the system; the next chart shows what happens after dogs and cats enter it.

Rehoming is the leading outcome, while a meaningful share is reclaimed.

This is the bridge from who is listed to how long they may wait: a successful outcome is not always an immediate one.

What influences how long pets wait?

Now that we know who makes up the adoption picture, we can look at the practical differences that shape time to home. Age, size and the type of organisation presenting a pet all matter.

Senior dogs have the longest median wait.
Larger dogs take longer to find a home; cats move faster at every tier.
Shelters show the fastest median pathway for both species.

What shapes adopter decisions?

The waiting-time patterns point towards a human explanation. What people hope to find, and what they hesitate to consider, can influence which animals are seen first.

Companionship stays close to 70% across every age bracket.

The desire for connection is shared across generations, creating a broad foundation for adoption.

Wanting a particular breed or a very young pet is the clearest hesitation.

These preferences can help explain why older, larger or less conventional pets face longer waits.

The adoption landscape is changing.

We have followed the pathway from access, to the animals being adopted, to the preferences that shape waiting time. The final question is whether the overall environment is becoming easier or harder for rescue organisations.

Every category sits lower in 2025 than in 2023.

The broad decline suggests the challenge is not limited to one species or one type of listing.

State-level shifts are uneven, so a national average hides local stories.

Local conditions matter, which brings the story back to the importance of place and rescue capacity.

The thread

Make the right pet easier to find.

Adoption outcomes reflect more than animal characteristics. They also reflect visibility, local rescue capacity and the expectations people bring to the search. Better matching, broader storytelling about older and larger animals, and stronger support for rescue organisations could help turn more listings into lasting homes.
