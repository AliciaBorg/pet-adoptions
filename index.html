What “Adoption” Actually Means

A common misconception is that simply aquiring a pet counts as adoption, including buying from a commercial breeder or pet shop. But actually, adoption has a specific meaning: giving a surrendered or rescued animal a new home.

In fact, formal adoptions account for only a minority of pet acquisitions. Survey results from 2024 show that just 36.2% of pets were acquired through a shelter, rescue or adoption agency.

Where Adoptions Are Happening in Australia

With formal adoptions accounting for a minority of pet acquisitions, where these adoptions are actually happening in Australia?

In 2025, Victoria recorded the highest PetRescue adoption rate, followed by Queensland and New South Wales. Rates are shown per 100,000 residents rather than as raw totals, so states with different population sizes can be compared fairly.

This begs the question: what's driving these geographical differences?

In 2025, PetRescue member organisations—spanning rescue groups, shelters, councils and vet clinics—were heavily concentrated along Australia's eastern states, being Victoria, Queensland and New South Wales.

They are also the states with the highest adoption rates, suggesting that the distribution of rescue organisations may influence adoption rates, although other factors are likely to contribute as well.

But geographical patterns only tell part of the story. To better understand adoption in Australia, it is also important to look at which animals are finding homes, and whether some species are adopted more often than others.

Victoria has a substantial number of PetRescue member organisations, making it a useful example for exploring how rescue organisations are distributed within a state.

The map shows Victoria's 163 member organisations, with one dot representing each location, with many clustered around Melbourne and others spread across regional centres and towns. This reveals how rescue organisations are geographically distributed across metropolitan and regional areas.

Who is being adopted?

Before looking at why some pets wait longer, it helps to see who makes up the adoption picture. Dogs and cats account for most recorded PetRescue adoptions, while other species make up a much smaller share.

For this reason, the following sections will largely focus on dogs and cats.

Dogs and Cats: What Influences How Long They Wait?

Now that we know dogs and cats make up the majority of the adoption picture, we can look at the differences that influence the time it takes for them to find a new home.

Type of organisation presenting, age and size all play a role in determining their time to adoption.

Shelters offer the fastest pathway for both species, with dogs and cats waiting roughly the same amount of time.
Senior dogs and cats wait significantly longer than younger animals, while cats consistently move through adoption faster than dogs.
For dogs, longer waits generally accompany larger size tiers, while cats consistently have shorter adoption times across every tier.

What shapes adopter decisions?

The waiting-time patterns hint at a human explanation: what people hope to find, and what they hesitate to consider, shapes which animals are seen first. When Australians do not consider rescuing a dog or cat, the clearest reasons relate to wanting a particular breed or a very young puppy or kitten. These preferences narrow the animals people are willing to consider and can help explain why older, larger or less conventional pets face longer waits. Other barriers, such as not thinking of rescue, appear less often by comparison.

On the other hand, companionship is the strongest motivation for adoption, staying close to 70% across every age bracket. The desire for connection is shared across generations, creating a broad foundation for rescue. This consistency suggests that people of all ages are drawn to the everyday relationship a pet can bring, rather than one narrow type of adopter.

Recent Rapid Changes in the Adoption Landscape.

We have followed the pathway from where adoptions happen, to which animals find homes, to the preferences that shape how long they wait. Together, these patterns show how visibility, access and adopter expectations influence the rescue journey.

But the overall direction is concerning: PetRescue adoption rates declined across dogs, cats and all pets between January and December 2025. The fall is broad rather than limited to one species, suggesting that rescue organisations are operating in a more difficult adoption environment.

As adoption activity weakens, making the right pet easier to find becomes increasingly important for turning listings into lasting homes.
